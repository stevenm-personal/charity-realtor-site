# Community page editorial and structural guide

This document is the dedicated reference for creating or substantially editing pages under `src/pages/communities/`. It works alongside `BRAND.md` and `AGENTS.md`. Preserve the Kansas Dusk visual direction, the site's established editorial voice, its mobile-first requirements, and every existing Fair Housing safeguard.

## Purpose

Community pages are evergreen local guides that support Charity Menefee's real estate website.

Their purpose is to:

- Give visitors genuinely useful information about a community.
- Demonstrate real local knowledge.
- Help buyers understand the types of property and practical considerations they may encounter.
- Support local SEO through useful, original content.
- Create natural entry points for more detailed future blog content.
- Make it easy to contact Charity without turning every section into a sales pitch.

Each page should feel as though it was written specifically for that city. It must not resemble a generic Realtor SEO page in which only the city name has been changed.

## Structural philosophy

Use a consistent backbone across community pages so the site feels cohesive, but do not force every community into an identical, rigid template.

Aim for approximately:

- 70% shared structure.
- 30% community-specific flexibility.

The information hierarchy should remain recognizable from page to page. Middle sections should follow what is genuinely interesting, useful, or important about the particular community.

## Typical community page flow

Use this flow as a flexible framework, not a mandatory checklist. Include, omit, reorder, or adapt middle sections when the community's actual story calls for it.

### 1. Hero

Purpose: give the visitor an immediate sense of place.

Typically include:

- `COMMUNITY GUIDE · [CITY]`
- A short, distinctive headline.
- One concise supporting statement.
- A genuinely recognizable local photograph when possible.

Do not overload the hero with factual detail. Photography should ideally feature something a local person would recognize instead of generic retail, chain businesses, or interchangeable suburban imagery.

Preserve visual room for both the text and the landmark. Use the shared per-image focal-position controls when needed, including separate desktop and mobile positions when the crop calls for them.

### 2. Getting oriented

This is usually the first section after the hero.

Purpose: explain at a high level where the community is and how it fits into Wichita-area geography.

Possible topics include:

- Location relative to Wichita.
- Whether it is its own municipality.
- Major access routes.
- General geographic context.
- Its physical relationship to surrounding communities.

Keep this section concise. Two or three paragraphs are usually enough. Do not overload it with housing, schools, amenities, or sales messaging.

### 3. Homes and housing

Because this is a real estate website, most community pages should explain what someone may encounter when searching for property there.

Useful topics can include:

- Older, established neighborhoods.
- Newer subdivisions and new construction.
- Different housing eras or styles.
- Lot patterns.
- Acreage or rural-edge properties where relevant.
- Patio homes, townhomes, or other housing forms when genuinely relevant.
- Meaningful differences between established and newer parts of town.

Keep the discussion factual and useful. Avoid vague claims such as:

- "Something for everyone."
- "Perfect for every lifestyle."
- "A great place to raise a family."

Do not fabricate current inventory, prices, market statistics, appreciation, or development status.

### 4. Local character and local knowledge

At least one middle section should ideally contain something genuinely specific to the community. This section is intentionally flexible.

Possible subjects include:

- A recognizable local landmark.
- A downtown or civic area.
- A recreation facility or park.
- A significant corridor.
- Locally important history.
- An interesting physical development pattern.
- A community event.
- A meaningful way the town has changed.
- Another fact or place residents would immediately recognize.

The goal is for someone familiar with the city to feel that the page actually knows the place. This section should vary substantially from one community to another.

### 5. Growth and what is changing

Use this section only when there is something meaningful and verified to discuss.

Possible topics include:

- Active residential development.
- Newer subdivisions.
- Expansion areas.
- Significant infrastructure or development affecting housing patterns.

Keep the treatment broad and reasonably evergreen. Briefly naming individual developments is often enough. Do not turn the guide into a detailed subdivision directory.

Use a layered content strategy:

1. The community guide briefly mentions current development.
2. A future blog post can provide a deeper look at that development.

Do not invent development phases, prices, builders, amenities, timelines, or availability. Verify time-sensitive development facts before publication.

### 6. Practical things to know

Use this section when it is helpful for the particular community.

Possible topics include:

- Municipal boundaries.
- Utilities.
- HOA considerations.
- Special assessments.
- Transportation and access.
- Property taxes.
- Acreage considerations.
- New-construction considerations.
- Floodplain considerations.
- School district or attendance boundaries.
- Other property-specific issues.

Do not force all of these topics onto every page. Choose only what is genuinely relevant.

### 7. Schools

Handle school information neutrally and carefully.

Acceptable factual information includes:

- The public school district or districts that serve an area.
- Links to official district resources.
- Reminders that attendance boundaries should be verified for a specific property.

Do not:

- Rank schools.
- Call schools good, bad, top, best, or use similar qualitative labels.
- Imply that one neighborhood is preferable because of its schools.
- Steer visitors toward or away from a location based on schools.
- Make demographic assumptions.

Prefer official school district and boundary sources whenever school information is included.

### 8. Useful questions

Most community pages should end their informational content with practical questions a buyer may want to investigate.

Make these questions specific to the community whenever possible instead of repeating an identical list across every page. They may address:

- City-limit boundaries.
- Newer development versus established construction.
- Access routes.
- HOA or special-assessment questions.
- Utilities.
- Lot characteristics.
- Property age.
- Building considerations.
- Community-specific property features.

This section should demonstrate thoughtful real estate guidance without becoming a sales pitch.

### 9. Contact call to action

End with one restrained, first-person call to action after the page has provided useful information.

Use first person when referring to Charity's assistance. Do not write third-person phrases such as "Charity can help." Do not repeat Realtor pitches throughout the body.

## Voice and point of view

Community pages are primarily informational and editorial.

- Use neutral informational language through most of the body.
- Use `you` when practical information is directed to the visitor.
- Use `I` selectively when Charity's personal help or perspective is genuinely relevant.
- Avoid corporate `we`.
- Avoid repeatedly referring to Charity by name in the third person.
- Avoid forced personal commentary in purely informational sections.
- Prioritize usefulness first and conversion second.

## Locality standard

Specificity matters. Prefer:

- Actual local landmarks.
- Real roads.
- Verified municipal features.
- Current, verified developments.
- Recognizable places.
- Locally relevant property considerations.

Avoid relying heavily on:

- Generic shopping references.
- National chain restaurants.
- Generic "great amenities" language.
- Clichés about suburban living.

A community page should contain enough specificity that replacing the city name with another city would make the page obviously incorrect.

## Photography

Use real local photography whenever practical.

Preferred subjects include:

- Recognizable landmarks.
- Civic features.
- Local parks and recreation.
- Skyline or downtown views.
- Distinctive local places.
- Community-specific development or landscape.

Avoid generic stock imagery when a meaningful local image is available. Different photographs may need different focal positions on desktop and mobile.

Do not force a landmark underneath large text when a better composition or lower image-and-text placement would showcase it more effectively. The most famous landmark does not automatically make the best hero. A broad city photograph may work better in the hero while the landmark supports a more focused section below.

Continue following the responsive image, Astro optimization, and asset-organization rules in `AGENTS.md`.

## Evergreen community guides and blog content

Keep community pages reasonably evergreen.

Use community pages for:

- Broad housing context.
- Geographic orientation.
- Local identity.
- Brief references to development.
- Practical property considerations.

Use blog posts for deeper or more time-sensitive subjects, including:

- Individual subdivisions.
- New development phases.
- Specific neighborhood profiles.
- Major construction projects.
- Detailed local updates.

Do not overload an evergreen community page with details that will quickly become stale.

## Fair Housing and steering

All Fair Housing rules in `AGENTS.md` remain in force.

Do not use language that describes or implies protected classes, ideal residents, or who belongs in a community. Avoid language such as:

- Safe.
- Family-friendly.
- Great for families.
- Ideal for young professionals.
- Perfect for retirees.
- Good neighborhood.
- Desirable because of demographics.
- Similar proxy language.

Do not infer the types of people who live in a community. Describe properties, geography, amenities, infrastructure, verified services, and other objective characteristics instead.

## Factual accuracy and research

Do not invent local facts.

If a requested detail is not already established in the project or explicitly supplied by the user:

- Do not guess.
- Do not fill the gap with plausible-sounding information.

Research and verify time-sensitive topics before presenting them as current facts. These include:

- Active developments.
- New construction.
- Subdivision status.
- Amenities.
- Municipal changes.
- Transportation projects.

Prefer primary and authoritative sources for factual verification, such as official municipal, county, school district, transportation, development, or project sources. Clearly separate stable facts from time-sensitive claims.

## Quality test

Before considering a community page complete, ask:

1. Does this page actually teach the visitor something about this specific community?
2. Would a local recognize the places or issues being discussed?
3. Could this copy be reused for another city simply by changing the city name? If yes, it is too generic.
4. Is the page useful even if the visitor never contacts Charity?
5. Is Charity's assistance available without being pushed into every section?
6. Are there any Fair Housing or steering concerns?
7. Are all current facts verified?
8. Does the page create clear opportunities for deeper future blog content without trying to contain all of it?
